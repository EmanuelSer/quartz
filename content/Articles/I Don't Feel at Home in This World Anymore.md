# I Don't Feel at Home in This World Anymore (2017)


Beginning as a reflexion on the absurdity of existence, It was perhaps the tone of the movie that got my attention in the first place and that might very well have been the intention of the movie’s writer-director, Macon Blair. 

Set in a suburban town, the movie follows the story of Ruth, a depressed woman who is struggling to make sense of her everyday life. Ruth is living a mundane and uneventful life and is feeling increasingly disconnected from the world around her. She is looking for something to provide her a sense of purpose and to bring some direction to her life. 
Things take a turn when Ruth discovers that her house has been robbed and she decides to take matters into her own hands. With the help of her eccentric neighbor, Tony, she embarks on a mission to track down the robbers and take back what is rightfully hers. 

he movie is filled with dark humour and is an excellent example of the sub-genre of dark comedy. It is also a reflection on the state of the modern world where people are increasingly feeling disconnected from each other and from their environment. 
The movie is an entertaining ride with some unexpected twists and turns. The performances from Melanie Lynskey and Elijah Wood are superb and the cinematography is well done. 
I Don’t Feel at Home in This World Anymore is an excellent movie that is worth watching for its unique take, a fun mix of comedy and existential dread. 
That alone would be enough for me to really enjoy the film but here there were a lot of pleasant surprises for a director who's work I've never found interesting before and even more so considering his following work on *"I Care a Lot"*.

This is a story of a couple of sad characters who's idea of good and bad it's essentially black and white. Even though they can rationally understand that there is a substantial difference someone who cuts in line and a murderer, they are unwilling to perceive the nuances and clump all the people they deem bad together in the same category: assholes.

However, in my opinion the greatest achievement of this film is it's construction of every sequence as it's own little story. It feels as though almost every sequence could be a short film on it's own. All gradually developing not only on their own right but also adding to the overall plot of the movie. Nothing seems to be there without a reason.

Sequences as short films. 
That's what I saw and loved.
