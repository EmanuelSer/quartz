I was already familiar with the plot of the book beforehand because I saw the **film adaptation** a couple of years earlier. That, however, didn't affect my experience with the story negatively, despite plot twists I might have ruined. But the way the book is written really is what sets it apart. So that even when I knew what was going to happen I was able to enjoy the way the author carries us to that point in the story... In a way exercising this idea of enjoying the **journey rather than the destination**.

It's easy to see then the value of the book over, and alongside, the film adaptation when it comes to the plot. The point of the story comes across rather neatly in both but the plot points presented in the book are a lot more telling about the general idea it's touching upon.

When you couple this with **Liu Zhenyun's incredible ability to flesh out characters in such detail that it almost comes alive**, you get this narrative style that's able to communicate so very much in just a few pages. He packs everything from cultural concepts, winks to other literary works, information about the characters' hierarchy and relationship, to simple plot devices in some paragraphs.

As an outsider looking into this book as a window to Chinese politics and culture I found it particularly interesting. I felt as if the author was trying to informing me specifically about all these characteristics and **peculiarities of Chinese society** with the excuse of a novel.

This all fades, however, when you begin to realize the way in which these characteristics are brought to light only in service of the story.

I'm talking, for instance, about **characters' flaws** that are there to exacerbate a certain aspect of their culture that can potentially become, with time and other factors, part of the problem the book is addressing.

Take the case of **Dong Xianfa in chapter 6**. First we are told the general connection this character will have with the main plot, then we are taken on a brief and peaceful journey through his professional career only to end up in the same spot with a greater understanding of the characters personality and motives so that we know what to expect of his interaction with the main character.

However, the example is hardly the only one. The author does this long and very engaging. **Sub-plots for every other character** making them feel a lot more human, three dimensional and real. Not to mention the **cinematic quality** of these descriptions and plot subdivisions. It truly feels like watching a movie. (Although I personally preferred a Wong Kar-wai film instead of the actual circular-framed adaptation).

I could go on and on and about how this character's mediocrity might be there to exacerbate Chinese obsession with success and how this, in turn, contributes to inflaming the case of Li Xuelian indirectly, about how that is but an example of core cultural concepts interfering with the effectiveness of their politics as being commented on by the author, but we need to move on to talk about the book's relationship with the film.

As a filmmaker student, that I unfortunately still technically am, I can't help but notice the **key differences made when translating this story from one medium to another**. For me, these differences come mainly in the form of **weight and balance**. Where the book can go from one character's perspective to the next in just a chapter—or in many cases even within the same chapter—the film takes a different approach, focusing entirely on the main character's journey and perception.

This means we, the audience, spend more time with her. I believe it is because of this that the author, who also worked on the script, decides to give **Liu a much more weighted motif for her protest**.

Finally, Ma's conclusion about the whole incident might be met as a reflection of the author's intention.

Wish I could make an adaptation of my own.
