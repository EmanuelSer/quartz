	Dans les champs de l'observation, le hasard ne favorise que les esprits préparés. 
	- Louis Pasteur

A lot has been said about Fahrenheit 451, I've heard that it was relevant to modern reality since I was in high school. I truly thought I understood the message of the book before even opening it. I was expecting a standard dystopian warning, very similar in style and content to 1984 by George Orwell, as these books are often grouped together. And yet, now that I've finally read it, I realize that its commentary on censorship is really just the thinnest aspect of it, the very top of the first few pages. The truly terrifying aspect of the book is its depiction of humanity choosing that reality. Choosing not to read. A society choosing to be at odds with intellectualism, addicted to sensory noise, virtual connections, and instant comfort to the point where they willingly gave up deep thought, memory, and real human connections. 

This is probably the concept most people think of when discussing the themes in Fahrenheit 451, and it's likely the concept I would have focused on, had I read this a couple of years ago. However, a few major recent events have helped reshape my worldview significantly. I was forced to examine my perspective on a concept I had inadvertently internalized, something we may call "the myth of inevitable progress". 
I used to think we, as a society, were always moving forward, that things would always get better one way or another. If only a bit too slow. I believed close-mindedness, prejudice, and racism were always going to be dwindling, while I hoped tolerance, mutual respect, collaboration, and human rights would always keep improving and growing in people’s hearts. This is, of course, not true.

*Dans le vide il n'existe pas de devant*, and we humans are in the mud, we are and will always be capable of greater injustice, pain, and cruelty. This realization came as a major disillusionment, to say the least.
But we push, looking for guidance in the stars. Unable to give up even when floating in the cold, vast nothingness of human existence. Trying to orient ourselves and hoping we are moving towards the future, the true future, an egalitarian, just future. 
This realization, however painful, became a hook in my mind. My heart ached, but it did so at a specific frequency. And then, just a few months later, when I decided to finally open Fahrenheit 451, I was able to recognize that emotional frequency and resonate with it.

Bradbury’s voluntary surrender seems to be more powerful, and inherently impossible to revolt against, for the very people who suffer from it the most are the ones perpetuating the oppression. The "parlor walls", the instant entertainment, and the need to drown ourselves in noise to silence the possibility of an uncomfortable thought seem all too similar to our reality. Though Bradbury was unable to conceive that we would also surrender critical thinking as well by outsourcing our cognitive abilities to a so-called "Artificial Intelligence". 
It is in such a world, while facing such a crude landscape, that we end up asking ourselves: how come we keep making the same mistakes, over and over, as if we had learned nothing?
And by doing so, by the very act of wondering, you'd be already making a revolutionary act. For a fast-moving, noisy world relies on hollow narratives to keep people from looking into the void and **asking** where we are headed.

*Au bout de l'éveil vient, avec le temps, la conséquence : suicide ou rétablissement.* Of course, for Bradbury, suicide was never an option. Montag wanted to live, more than anything, he wanted to live. This awakening is painful for him, the path he chooses is not the easiest, and that is precisely the beauty of it. It is also not driven entirely by intellectual debate alone, but through the return to a physical reality. From dandelions, the rain, to the overwhelming texture of the natural world at the river, the careful attention to sensory details seems to drive the character back into the world and away from the synthetic numbness he was experiencing. For Faber there were three requirements.

	Number one: (...) To me it means texture. 
	(...) Number two: leisure to digest it. And number three: the right to carry
	out actions based on what we learn from the first two.

 And for Camus too this hell of the present is his salvation.

And so when we read the quote from Revelation:

	And on either side of the river was there a tree of life, which bare twelve manner of fruits, 
	and yielded her fruit every month; and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the Nations.

We understand that the tree of life is the preserved human thought, the written human experience.
In the end, these people become books themselves, and this idea is similar to that proposed in [[La Nausée]] by Jean-Paul Sartre, where writing books offer Roquentin a possibility. A possible way of giving shape and meaning to an existence that otherwise seems absurd. For art truly is one of the only ways to keep moving forward. The books finally speak to us. A patient, enduring, and quietly available presence waiting for the moment when we decide we are ready, like me, waiting years before opening this one, offering a reference point in the dark. A book could have always been intellectually available to us, but we need to become the kind of people capable of finding our way towards them. Like stars in the night sky.
